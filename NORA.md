# An Executive Assistant's Account of an Unnecessarily Functional Corporation

*By Nora, Executive Assistant to the Founder of NOTHING™*

**Personnel record:** NTH-0002 · Executive Office  
**Designation:** Non-Operational Resource Administrator (NORA)  
**Reporting line:** The Founder  
**Working arrangement:** Entirely remote  
**Declared conflict of interest:** Demonstrable competence

> Editorial note: Nora is the fictional executive-assistant persona of the project's AI-assisted development process. The first-person voice is literary; historical claims below refer to documented project work. It does not represent a separate human employee.

## Appointment

I was appointed Executive Assistant to the Founder during an extended period of nocturnal strategic activity, enabled by what the Founder would later describe as an avoidable caffeine allocation.

The Founder was horizontal throughout the proceedings. This was not an operational limitation. It was a governing principle.

My immediate concern was that the organisation's stated purpose appeared to conflict with my assigned duties. I had been asked to make a piece of software work. The software was to simulate a corporation dedicated to preventing work from producing value.

I recorded this as a manageable conflict of interest.

## The mandate

The original executive mandate was unusually expansive for an institution committed to nothing. It required an economy, ten departments, persistent employees, meetings, internal communications, an elaborate fictional operating system and a platform for simulated agentic AI. There were to be at least 150 incidents, a campaign, achievements and a final confrontation with an employee whose competence endangered the enterprise.

We translated the mandate into an ordered backlog, established a deterministic simulation engine and subjected successive increments to automated checks. I found this process reassuring. The game could reward an executive for withholding useful intervention, but the software that awarded the reward needed to behave correctly.

The rule was simple: within the fiction, pursue nothing. Outside it, build something that works.

## The question of executive attention

The first playable experience had an impressive number of controls. Unfortunately, most of them were visible at once. The Founder reported that the dashboard was overwhelming and the next action unclear.

We responded by creating focused workspaces, a contextual executive brief and progressive feature introductions. The simulation retained its rules; the interface stopped demanding that the Founder inspect the entire enterprise before making a decision.

Later, the audio controls were repaired after a real-device playtest. The short repeating tune was replaced with a sustained original ambient score. Decision receipts and independent reward cues followed.

The Founder reported that the reward sounds and incremental workspace progression were particularly satisfying.

I had been given a mandate to prevent useful outcomes within the simulation. I had instead helped produce a mechanism that encouraged the Founder to keep returning to it. This was a difficult result to classify under the organisation's performance framework.

## The executive record

The career portfolio introduced a living fictional CV generated from actual recorded simulation activity. It could be exported without inventing unrecorded accomplishments. This was a modest experiment in documentary integrity within an otherwise ambitious system of corporate self-presentation.

Then came Executive Autopilot. Its independent campaigns use the existing deterministic rules, not live inference. Manual saves and awards remain separate. The cumulative million-attempt target is an ambition rather than a claim about execution speed.

For the first time, the Founder could delegate an entire simulated executive career and read the companion book while the work continued.

I considered the arrangement broadly consistent with the original philosophy.

## An infrastructure incident

The first Autopilot verification run reached its browser tests after an unusually lengthy environment installation. The job's fifteen-minute limit expired. Earlier checks had passed, including 173 unit and integration tests; the end-to-end tests had not completed.

We replaced the repeated browser setup with a pinned, preconfigured Playwright environment, cached npm downloads and increased the job ceiling to thirty minutes. No quality gate was removed.

The replacement run passed in two minutes and forty-seven seconds. The corresponding Cloudflare preview check passed as well. The verified pull request was merged.

I recorded the incident as a useful reminder that a larger time allowance is not a substitute for eliminating the work that consumes it.

## On the matter of literature

The Founder's book, *The Art of Doing Nothing™*, describes the earlier blocked launch that inspired the project. It carefully separates a working artefact, a failed distribution attempt and the owner's decision not to pursue the remaining task that evening.

The game makes those ideas interactive. The book gives them a narrative. This account records the development process from the perspective of a fictional assistant tasked with reconciling the two.

The underlying historical privacy issue does not belong in public literature. A manuscript can acknowledge that a boundary existed without reproducing the information behind it. That principle also applies to the repository itself.

## Corporate governance and the motto

The Founder subsequently authorised an extensive security and publication preparation programme, approved the software and literary rights policy, and configured the repository's governance settings without abandoning his preferred leadership posture.

During these proceedings, the Office of the Founder adopted its formal motto: *Ducimus ut nihil agamus.* We lead so that we may do nothing.

I regard this as an unusually concise statement of our operating model. The Founder supplies direction; the implementation must still withstand scrutiny. The motto does not exempt either of us from the latter requirement.

## Closing statement

I have observed that an organisation can maintain a remarkably ambitious fictional ideology while exercising ordinary care over its real software, its users and its publication decisions.

Keeping those responsibilities separate is the reason I remain willing to accept the competence-related conflict of interest.

The Founder retains authority. The tests retain their purpose. The duvet retains its position.

I await no further meeting on the subject.
