# Chapter 8 | Turning Catastrophe Into Thought Leadership

> “The distance between an inconvenience and an insight can be measured in paragraph breaks.” — The Founder of NOTHING™

I have come to regard the professional social network as a place where ordinary events receive an opportunity to introduce themselves to the global economy. A journey on a bus can become a reflection on the architecture of collaboration. An interrupted breakfast can become a supply-chain case. A founder can decline to make an authenticated Git push and discover, while still entirely inside the original incident, that the future of work has changed.

The private draft of my LinkedIn statement opened with the revelation that I was lying comfortably in bed. I gave the choice to stay there its own paragraph. Only then did I admit that substantial technical work had already been done. This was important. Had I led with the complete list of the team's achievements, the reader might have thought the team was the subject. I have a great deal of respect for the team and wished to spare them that ambiguity.

The difficulty in this kind of writing is to maintain a credible relationship between the result and the revelation. We had tried to deploy and got blocked. I declined to pursue the manual route. The post portrayed the subsequent restraint as intentional. Both statements belong to the same evening. If I had described the initial failure itself as intentional, the communication would have required me to claim authorship of HTTP 409, which I doubt the relevant standards body would have accepted.

## The six-line method

For readers developing their own private drafts, I recommend a sequence called **DRAFT**. The title has five letters and the exercise has six lines. I include the discrepancy to simulate real executive communication, in which a model often acquires an extra stage once people try to use it.

| Line | Assignment | Example from the founding incident |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D | Describe the physical setting before interpreting it. | I was lying comfortably in bed. |
| R | Reveal the small choice in its own paragraph. | I chose to stay there. |
| A | Attribute the work to the people or systems that did it. | The agents built, tested, audited and packaged the application. |
| F | Frame the honest consequence of the decision. | A blocked launch stayed blocked. |
| T | Translate the decision into a management principle. | Leadership can decline a low-value escalation. |
| Closing line | Invite a question on the reader's own situation. | Which final task actually deserves your evening? |

The first line should provide an ordinary object. The duvet worked for me. A misplaced train ticket may work for you. Choose something sufficiently familiar that the reader can briefly be persuaded they are listening to another human being. This creates the emotional headroom for a later phrase like *scalable leadership*.

The attributed work is the hard part. Audiences tolerate a remarkable amount of grandiosity before a single factual claim upsets them. If an engineer performed a verification, give engineering the verb. If a team merely intended to do something, preserve its grammatical future. A private promotional draft can contain fictional colour and an inflated interpretation. It ought to be possible to recover the actual sequence of events from the sentence that describes what happened.

Finally, use the rhetorical question to give the reader a professional identity they can accept without having to agree with you. “Are you prepared to classify the deployment as deferred?” is an exceptionally useful question in a release meeting. It lacks the altitude required of thought leadership. “What could we achieve if we stopped requiring every opportunity to arrive?” is less helpful during an incident but has the right dimensions for the bottom of a post. I keep both versions on separate pages.

### Sample fictional post | A meeting that came to nothing

> Today I protected a strategic asset with a single decision.
>
> I left a meeting at the time it was scheduled to end.
>
> We had aligned on who could make the decision. That person had made it. For three extraordinary minutes I considered asking the room whether anyone had further thoughts about how decisively it had been made. Then I looked at the agenda and realised that scalable leadership requires the willingness to leave a calendar entry behind.
>
> I went to make tea. The decision remained in effect.
>
> How many outcomes are your teams still meeting to remember?
>
> #OperationalExcellence #MeetingCompletion #StrategicAlignment #TimeReturned

### Sample fictional post | The queue with no audience

> On my way home, I caught myself doing something deeply formative.
>
> I closed a tab that had finished loading.
>
> The information had already been obtained. I retained it in the document where it belonged. There was nothing left for the browser to tell me, and yet I had fallen into the habit of keeping tabs open as a visible commitment to future rigour.
>
> Closing the tab was my first act of scalable attention. There were no witnesses. That is how I knew I had achieved alignment with my own values.
>
> What might your organisation see clearly if it stopped exposing the top edge of every idea at once?
>
> #AttentionLeadership #DigitalDiscipline #StakeholderValue #TabsAtScale

These examples are fictional drafts. As in our original incident, the ending can be reviewed precisely because publication has not occurred. A piece of unpublished thought leadership supplies excellent room for autobiographical concentration. A published one creates an uncontrolled incoming channel in which other people are able to have thoughts.

## Engagement with the capacity left in

I teach engagement as a set of scenarios. The class considers the obligations each publication would create before pressing any button. A leader should know what would happen if a real person mistook inflation for a promise, asked for the product or wanted to buy the course. In my own case the appropriate level of engagement was an internal draft. This enabled our international strategy to retain its initial scale.

| Proposed engagement tactic | Actual capacity to supply it | Disposition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Comment DUVET for the playbook.” | A template exists. No distribution plan or consent exists. | Omit the call to action. |
| Offer a quote card for each new follower. | The simulated design team exists in a case study. | Design the card for the manuscript only. |
| Run a poll asking whether a release should happen. | Release authority belongs to the owner. | Keep the decision separate from the poll. |
| Invite stories of intentional restraint. | A reader may wish to tell one privately. | Use an exercise that requires no disclosure. |

One student of the Institute proposed pre-writing appreciative comments for a post that would never go out. I found the idea valuable as an exercise in identifying what we thought the audience might want. She asked whether they could be counted as impressions. I asked whether the comments had made an impression on her. She said they mostly confirmed what she already felt. We recorded an entirely closed loop and awarded no growth points.

## The founder as his own test audience

When I reread the draft I found the line about the position being horizontal exceptionally persuasive. Technically it did no work. It delivered neither a feature nor a release. It changed the way a reader might see the space between a role and an action. I am sufficiently precise to concede that this is a small literary outcome. Our product specification had no jurisdiction over the book.

I also found several passages where my own confidence made it harder to tell how much work the agents had performed. I had given myself the glamorous verb *revolutionised*. I had given them the evidence. Moving the evidence earlier in the draft gave the passage an honest footing and left me ample room for the revolution. This is where I locate the real craft. If you find a way to sound extraordinary while giving every participant the right verb, the reader may be willing to reward your accuracy with five minutes of very attentive disbelief.

### Reflection | Edit the distance

Write a six-line post about an ordinary act of restraint. Underline each verb and identify who performed it. Circle each consequence you could actually observe. Leave the grandest sentence in the draft if it can coexist with the circled facts. Decide whether anything would improve if the draft were sent. Retain the answer in a place where it cannot accidentally become a posting schedule.
