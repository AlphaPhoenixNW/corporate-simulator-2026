# Chapter 3 | Strategic Executive Non-Intervention™

> “If an intervention can be avoided, its preliminary meeting can usually be avoided at a still earlier stage.” — The Founder of NOTHING™

A person can remain inactive for many reasons. They may have lost the instructions. They may be unable to reach the person who wrote them. They may have confused *reviewed* with *approved* in a set of notes that uses the words interchangeably. I wanted a leadership system capable of recognising my own more sophisticated condition. I had the instructions, understood the blockage and was unwilling to assign the evening to it.

I define **Strategic Executive Non-Intervention™** as the deliberate preservation of executive attention through the elimination of unnecessary operational participation. That definition protects the word *deliberate* from two colleagues it should never have been left alone with: *automatically* and *indefinitely*. Automatically avoiding a question usually means the question will be asked again. Indefinite deferral is sometimes appropriate, as it was for our release, but the person holding the authority should understand what would cause the subject to reopen. In my case the condition was a fresh owner decision. Nobody had to wake me up to check whether the passing of midnight counted.

The act of declining a task has historically suffered from poor presentation. A person who declines gets asked why, as though participation is neutral and non-participation must submit a business case. I invite the leader to reverse the order of presentation. Before the proposed intervention, ask what changes if you do it. Ask who asked for the change. Ask what authority you exercise by agreeing. Only then consider whether your presence is required. A room accustomed to your attendance may experience several seconds of intolerable clarity.

## The four conditions

I teach leaders to check four conditions before invoking the method. I call the card **STAY** because it is possible to consult it without needing to rise.

| Test | Question I ask | Result on launch night |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope | What immediate action is proposed? | Authenticate a Git push and initialise the private destination. |
| Trigger | What would make it timely or necessary? | An active release decision with an available operator. |
| Authority | Who may decide on the immediate action and the later publication? | An authorised operator can push. The owner decides publication. |
| Yield | Which gains and consequences do I accept for acting or deferring? | A push begins verification. Deferral preserves the evening and leaves public access unavailable. |

The third row assigns separate authority for a narrow operation and the later decision it might make possible. This is a common source of trouble. Teams sometimes hand a former decision-maker the broadest credentials imaginable because a narrow permission would require another conversation. Leaders sometimes treat a completed mechanical step as evidence that the wider decision has been made. These efficiencies are purchased on credit. The invoice usually arrives when someone asks who approved the next thing that happened.

I took the opposite course. I left the permission boundary intact and accepted the absence of a release. This was substantially more defensible than proposing a clever route around authentication from under the duvet. It had the additional virtue of requiring exactly the degree of Git access already available to me in that posture.

## Retain accountability

My most advanced exercise concerns the pronoun *I*. In a large organisation the passive voice enables extraordinary muscular feats. A schedule can move without anyone lifting it. A deadline can be missed in a room occupied exclusively by people who were there at the time. An initiative can enter a period of review as though it had set off down a corridor unattended.

I use the first person for the executive decision. I did not pursue the remaining manual operation. I accepted indefinite postponement. I retained the responsibility to reopen the subject. If your own example contains one of the following phrases, examine whether its grammatical subject has a vote on the matter.

- “It was determined that intervention could be deferred.”
- “The timeline experienced a change in executive availability.”
- “A decision to pause was identified.”
- “Leadership was present in a reduced physical capacity.”

I admit some affection for the fourth. It is an observation of considerable elegance. It does very little to tell an engineer whether to wait around for the push.

A team deserves enough information to stop working when the leader has decided to stop the work. This is the operating benefit I attach to clarity. Telling everybody there is a strategic review, while privately intending to go to sleep, leaves them employed by your silence. A recorded deferral frees them to close the task without having to interpret your movements. The duvet is a leadership resource because its obligations have an exceptionally visible perimeter. The people outside it should have a clear statement of their own.

### A case from the Institute | The silent signature

A fictional chair named Nisha is offered a document requiring her signature before a pilot can commence. The lead says the pilot cannot start without her approval. This is true. The lead then says that because the pilot is ready, approval is an administrative detail. At this point the leader has to decide whether the detail is doing any work.

Nisha asks one question. What happens to people who believe the pilot will start this afternoon if I defer it? The team identifies nobody outside the room. She asks what new evidence is expected tomorrow. The team identifies one outstanding permission. Nisha writes a short deferral, names the evidence that would reopen it and tells the team to reclaim the slot. The pilot remains unstarted. Nobody spends the afternoon waiting for the printer to express a view.

On a second run we give the students a different fact. A real obligation has already been made for that afternoon. This time Nisha has to act or arrange a responsible alternative. A few students argue that the course would be more consistent if Nisha also remained silent. I explain that leaving a duty unfulfilled is an ambition available to anyone. Strategic Executive Non-Intervention™ requires a person capable of reading the duty and admitting when it applies.

## The prestige of a declined invitation

Most senior calendars contain a service call disguised as a choice. The leader is asked to attend so that somebody senior can confirm that a senior person has attended. Their arrival allows a meeting to begin, sets the tone and enables the minutes to thank them. Under this model, whole teams live in a state of suspended animation between arrivals. The leader, finely dressed and deeply tired, mistakes their ability to wake a meeting for evidence that the meeting needed to exist.

The better approach is to ask the team to bring a question that requires a decision. It can still be an awkward question. It can be uncomfortable. I am happy to undertake discomfort when it has been assigned a role. The range of things I can now decline with confidence is wide enough to be liberating. The range of things I actually owe is small enough to notice.

I developed this discipline well after I had already decided to stay in bed. I record that sequence as an advantage. An institution that only recognises strategy written in advance forfeits an entire class of brilliant decisions ordinary adults make when they can see their slippers and see no sensible reason to use them.

### Reflection | Practise a clear deferral

Choose a proposed intervention with no pressing obligation. Fill in STAY. Write a three-sentence decision naming the action, the consequence of deferral and the reopening trigger. Send it only to people who would otherwise wait. If you find yourself proposing a meeting to discuss your three sentences, decide which of the three statements has failed to do its work.
