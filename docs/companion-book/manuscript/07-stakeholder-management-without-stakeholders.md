# Chapter 7 | Stakeholder Management Without Stakeholders

> “Every audience deserves consideration. Some are best served by the continued absence of correspondence.” — The Founder of NOTHING™

Until the launch failed, I had given remarkably little thought to the people who might want NOTHING™. The word *public* had allowed me to imagine one general recipient of a fine piece of nothing. Once there was an incident, that recipient divided into recognisable classes. There were prospective customers, investors, partners, developers, operational teams and enthusiasts. Several were international by the time I had finished imagining them. None had entered a commercial relationship.

This is the administrative power of an apology. You start with an acknowledgement of what happened. You then ask who may have been affected. When the category *affected* is suitably broad, you can populate a whole organisation around the things that might have happened to people who were never there. I decided that if the company had no actual stakeholders in these categories, it could at least give the fictional ones a much better grade of apology than organisations routinely send to living customers.

The formal draft began with the straightforward admission that the product had failed to become publicly available. It stated the grievance in a sentence I still consider a good example of enterprise writing.

> “We recognise that many stakeholders were promised absolutely nothing. Regrettably, we have been unable to deliver even that.”

This worked because it acknowledged two separate states. People may have expected an opportunity to experience absence. What they received was no opportunity. In practice both involved a good deal of absence. From an audience perspective the presentation was very different.

## The fictional stakeholder map

| Imagined audience | What they reserved | Nature of the inconvenience |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prospective customers | A little attention and the possibility of a local download | They had to experience nothing without our specifications. |
| Investors who committed no capital | A willingness to discuss the shape of a return | They had no financial instrument with which to record its absence. |
| Enterprise partners without agreements | A hypothetical business relationship | The failure preceded even the parts where the terms become inconvenient. |
| Developers | A lovely empty change list | They could neither contribute functionality nor take credit for its omission. |
| Operational teams | A blank maintenance calendar | They had to preserve the schedule without benefit of a formal handover. |
| Enthusiasts | An international enthusiasm for outcome-neutral computing | They lacked a public location at which to assemble around the concept. |

These positions do not imply a user base. I am particularly careful with *investors*. There is a considerable difference between saying that no investors committed capital and thanking a group of people for their patience in a way that makes it sound as though there is a cap table somewhere. Where money did not change hands because no relationship existed, I refrain from suggesting that sophisticated demand was restrained by prudent market timing. That wording belongs in a more advanced chapter.

The operations team also requires sensitivity. In some companies the folks who keep the systems live are the very people least likely to receive a formal apology until something dramatic happens. Our fictional staff maintained no infrastructure. This was no excuse to forget them. We acknowledged how difficult it can be to reserve a window in which nothing needs to occur and then discover that the intended lack of occurrence has been cancelled. I imagined them accepting the apology in a spirit of professional solidarity, which had the benefit of requiring no reply.

## The anatomy of a corporate apology

A good apology has four parts I can name without holding a communications workshop. Describe the event. Name the affected group accurately. Accept responsibility for what belongs to you. State a next step only if you can stand behind it.

There is a stage between the second and third parts when an executive may panic and insert *any inconvenience*. These words make the speaker sound as though the event took place in a building with unreliable central heating and is still being investigated. Our apology stated that the inconvenience was grave. We could afford this level of confidence because the audiences were part of the satire and the letter plainly said so. Real communications demand that somebody speak to the people who bore the cost. Fictional communications permit a very high proportion of ceremonial concern.

I concluded the draft with a commitment to learning nothing, improving nothing and making no promises about future delivery. It pleased me to put the promise boundary in the last paragraph. There is a conventional pattern in which an apology closes by saying the organisation is deeply committed to making the event right. This obligates somebody to design a new project while the apology is still being proofread. I had recognised the inconvenience already. Nobody had to start a second initiative in order to make my remorse grammatical.

### A case from the Institute | The empty subscriber list

A fictional company announces internally that it will postpone an optional newsletter. Its editor has acquired a strong commitment to transparency. She writes an apology for subscribers and asks three colleagues to approve an extended FAQ. A fourth asks whether the list has been created. It has, but it contains zero addresses. The editor raises the possibility that people who would have subscribed were nevertheless harmed by the postponement. The fourth colleague concedes a speculative injury and asks how the editor proposes to notify them without first creating the thing she has postponed.

The editor records the decision on the newsletter plan and stops. The following day she observes that nobody has complained. We instruct her to preserve both facts: she knows the subscriber list was empty, and she has no basis for claiming that her excellent handling of the incident improved audience sentiment. Students find this a difficult end to a case study. There is nobody left to deliver the commendation. I sometimes write one internally, so they can complete the module.

## The restraint to keep a draft

An apology can be internally useful even where it will never be sent. It forces the founder to represent what the audience could have wanted. It makes a status comprehensible without the language of milestones. It can also prevent a senior figure from claiming that a period of absence was recognised and admired by several entire classes of people.

We kept ours in the private record. No financial statement followed. Nobody was entered on a public mailing list in order to receive the good news that our relationship would continue to be imaginary. Of all our communications decisions, this one was adapted most precisely to the actual size of the audience.

I do use the apology in training. I tell the class that the job of stakeholder management is to maintain honest relations with the people involved. If the people exist, this may require work. If the people are fictional, the job is more finely balanced. One needs sufficient imagination to write them a generous apology and sufficient judgement to leave it in the building.

### Reflection | Name the audience

Write the opening of an apology for a project that failed to become available. List each actual recipient. Under that list, make a second list of audiences your language has merely brought into existence. Decide which group is owed a message and what you can truthfully promise it. Give the second group access to a perfectly legible draft that remains exactly where it is.
