# Chapter 4 | The Horizontal Leadership Model™

> “I assessed the matter from four positions. Three were an unnecessary use of the spine.” — The Founder of NOTHING™

Most management models are presented on a vertical surface by a person standing next to it. This produces a number of unconscious assumptions. The person standing is in charge. Progress goes upwards unless the chart concerns spending. The more successful a career becomes, the fewer chairs its owner is permitted to occupy in front of an audience. I spent part of my early leadership life accepting these conventions without ever asking which one of them had made a decision.

I want to rehabilitate posture as information. Over the course of an ordinary working day you can watch the body receive operational demands. A person stands when they are about to assist. They sit when the request has acquired a document. They recline when somebody else has obtained access to the document and appears able to read it. The last movement, into the horizontal, occurs when the possibility of personal participation is finally brought under control.

These are fictional organisational postures. I can conduct a vertical meeting while entirely prone and I have seen an exceptionally horizontal decision made by somebody carrying a box. Physical position remains a useful way to teach the model. In my own founding case the literal and organisational postures coincided to a degree unlikely to be repeated without careful preparation.

## Four positions, one operating environment

| Posture | Organisational reading | What I look for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vertical | Operationally overcommitted | Leaders personally doing work that has a clear owner elsewhere |
| Seated | Strategically uncertain | Leaders convening others to discover whether there is a decision to make |
| Reclined | Approaching leadership maturity | The work has an owner, and the executive can describe the remaining boundary |
| Horizontal | Executive optimisation achieved | An informed decision is recorded and the leader can withdraw without leaving people waiting |

The vertical leader feels useful because requests visibly move through their hands. They show someone where the file is, forward the file to somebody who already has it, read two paragraphs aloud and offer to reply to a question everybody received simultaneously. This is a busy posture. Over time it produces a team whose understanding of the process consists largely of knowing where the leader stands.

The seated leader has discovered the chair and the agenda. These are important discoveries. In place of handling each request, they assemble the requests in bulk and invite a cross-section of the organisation to watch them draw a line under *Any Other Business*. The seated leader can preserve critical dependencies for months. Things that could have become decisions in ten minutes survive as standing items, a label that does nothing to improve the posture of anybody involved.

Reclining asks more of the leader. The work must be intelligible enough to delegate. It helps to know who can act, what they can do and how they will report the consequences. The leader must be able to receive a relevant fact without personally reenacting the process that produced it. This is a difficult adjustment for anyone who has become the most expensive confirmation dialog in their organisation. An early symptom of recovery is hearing a verified result and replying, “Good,” with no additional action attached.

The horizontal posture is reserved for decisions that have reached an honest resting point. The decision can be to proceed by delegation, to defer with a stated consequence or to close work that has served its purpose. The essential quality is that the leader can be absent while the decision remains true. In the case of NOTHING™, the record still said no launch the next morning. I regard this preservation of state without my continuous participation as one of the first demonstrations of the model.

## The duvet as a controlled resource

There was a time when I considered the duvet an ordinary household article. I now recognise three properties that make it an unusually well-governed asset.

First, it protects a defined area. Its boundary is visible. A request must cross that boundary to reach the executive, and the executive can tell the difference between an item inside it and one outside it. This is more than I can say for several shared inboxes.

Second, it is adjustable. You may raise it to admit a decision of unusual importance without surrendering the entire area. A decision of middling importance may be handled through a modest aperture near the shoulder. If the decision is really a request to acknowledge something you acknowledged earlier, the duvet can remain at its existing height.

Third, the duvet reveals whether a process is designed around needless movement. A process that cannot be explained while the executive is comfortably covered probably needs an owner, a shorter explanation or a fit person prepared to carry it to the other side of the room. In training I call these the **Coverage Operating Limits**, or COL. A team presenting outside COL should be able to point to the exact authority for which it requires a change in posture.

This model can be abused by an executive who raises the duvet simply to avoid bad news. I require the exception to be conspicuously small. A request that identifies an existing duty or actual harm can cross any fabric boundary. The duvet is a resource for securing attention. It is too light to be used as a defence against accountability.

### A case from the Institute | The travelling chair

In one fictional company, the managing director buys a reclining office chair and announces a horizontal transformation. On the first day she attends eleven meetings. In three she personally reassigns minor tasks. In two she insists upon reading the display from her chair while a junior employee holds it at an angle. By the afternoon an employee is making a small riser so she can operate her keyboard without sitting up. The chair has succeeded as furniture. As a management intervention it has financed an extra pair of hands.

A colleague then tries an experiment. She brings the director only the two decisions for which written authority is required. All other work is assigned to its original owners. One decision is signed. The other is deferred with a condition for reopening. For the first time, the director can recline without the laptop, display, keyboard or employee moving in sympathy. The Institute acknowledges both chairs. We certify the second arrangement.

## A transition you can observe

Before claiming maturity, write down what happens after you leave. Is somebody waiting to discover what you meant? Is a task suspended until you return? Does everybody have the same understanding of who can act? If the answers are poor, adjust the work. Training an entire office to stretch out across the nearest available surface merely adds a facilities problem.

I sometimes draw the transition as a sequence on a board:

| Start | Required change | Evidence you can inspect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I personally touch everything | Give routine work an owner | Questions go to the owner |
| I convene to understand everything | Ask for a precise decision | The decision is recorded |
| I see only the remaining boundary | Give a clear instruction or defer | The team knows whether to continue |
| I am available by exception | Let ordinary work proceed | My absence creates no new task |

This sequence can be traversed seated if it helps. The point of the physical model is to notice a dependency. Once you have removed the dependency, your posture becomes a matter for you and the chair. I call that progress. I may even confirm it without getting up.

### Reflection | Trace the dependency

Choose one task that always waits for your presence. Find the specific authority, information or reassurance that makes it wait. Move the first two to a durable decision or a named operator. Examine how often the third requires an actual meeting. Draw the resulting process on the smallest sheet of paper available.
