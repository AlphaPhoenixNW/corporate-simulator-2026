# Chapter 2 | The Productivity Lie

> “Industry has spent centuries increasing the output of a working hour. I propose that we finally give the hour a say in the matter.” — The Founder of NOTHING™

My first difficulty in describing the events of 25 November was that every available word for my decision appeared to belong to a moral judgement. To finish was admirable. To leave something unfinished suggested a defect of character. To be productive was to move one object, by some quantity, from a place where it could not benefit anybody into a place where its benefits could be counted. I wanted to know who had drawn up this arrangement and why they had neglected to invite the people whose evenings would fund it.

Take the expression *a productive evening*. It suggests an evening capable of yielding a dividend. I have had evenings in which I cooked, answered three emails, investigated an error, washed a cup and read a conversation I had already read that afternoon. The case for their productivity could be made entirely through the presentation of evidence. At bedtime I would still have been entitled to ask what any of it was for. I rarely did. By the time I came to ask, I had already collected a list and the list had acquired the appearance of an answer.

There is an old convenience in judging effort by what can be photographed. An empty desk looks as though its owner has run out of work. A surface covered in papers looks as though its owner needs funding. A day with an unbroken white rectangle in the calendar looks precarious. Fill the rectangle with four adjacent appointments and you have a professional whose judgement everybody is suddenly willing to wait three weeks to obtain.

This is the productivity lie as I understand it. We assign value to the quality of leaving a mark, long before we inspect what the mark represents. An industry that treats traces of activity as evidence of impact hands an exceptional opportunity to anyone prepared to make the traces very handsome.

## Outcome-Neutral Performance™

My alternative starts with a requirement that will seem severe. I ask the leader to state the expected change in the world before asking anybody to work. If the answer is *none*, write *none*. Do this while the page is empty, before the headings about dependencies and impact. Once a team sees that there is a section headed *benefits*, it may feel obliged to produce something to put there. A good template can create work at almost the same rate as a poor decision.

I call the resulting field **Outcome-Neutral Performance™**. It is the capacity to produce credible evidence that a specified outcome remained exactly where you intended to leave it. A lender might care how much money was repaid. A railway passenger might care whether the train arrived. In those circumstances a change is required, and the principles of this book would need to wait for the next service. Within the approved perimeter of NOTHING™, zero was the design requirement. The application gave an unambiguous report, terminated and left the business ledger in pristine condition.

This was unexpectedly difficult to explain to a fictional prospective customer during an exercise at the Institute. I gave the reader playing the customer a screen containing three stages and a clear confirmation. She asked how to export the results. I told her there were no results to export. She said she would still appreciate a button. We briefly considered adding a button that did nothing, but the existence of a button would have raised the expectation that pressing it would accomplish nothing in a more convenient way. I felt that this would create a second product line before we had distributed the first.

A passing result was easy to demonstrate. We could start the process, observe it closely and return to the same practical position as before. The important point is that the evidence survived the exercise. Without the evidence, a person might think we had simply made no attempt. High-performance non-delivery depends on making the attempt legible at the appropriate level.

## The four ledgers

I use four imaginary ledgers to review an activity. They often contain different entries. I regard any meeting in which a person tries to reconcile all four at once as a source of employment for the next meeting.

| Ledger | What we record | NOTHING™ example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Activity | Things the team did | Implemented and tested the runtime |
| Evidence | Things a reviewer can inspect | Results, source archives and an exact transcript |
| Effect | Things that changed for the intended audience | Public availability did not change |
| Obligation | Things still owed or explicitly deferred | Authenticated import deferred by the owner |

Think of the activity ledger as a witness with an unusually good memory and a personality unsuited to questions of value. It remembers that a meeting took place. It remembers the name of the meeting and that next Tuesday the same people will attend one entitled *Continue Discussion*. That proves both lots of time have been spoken for. The evidence ledger may contain a good decision. The effect ledger can still contain an empty line.

The obligation ledger is the one executives should read before making a speech. A declared null outcome does not wipe away an obligation somebody accepted. In the incident I could waive my own plans for the evening, leave the legitimate work credited to engineering and preserve the authority gate. I could not have chosen to turn a safety obligation into a gesture of leadership. The Institute reserves the right to terminate any case study that contains a person stranded at a station.

### A case from the Institute | The Quarterly Lighting Committee

Consider an illustrative office in which the leadership team receives complaints about a bright meeting room. Someone proposes a steering committee to assess the merits of moving the meeting. To provide continuity, the committee convenes in the bright room. The discomfort generates meetings of excellent attendance. It also generates an urgent need for subgroups. One group compares blinds. A second inspects patterns of light. A third examines the strategic advantages of bringing the other two together.

An ordinary manager solves the problem by moving the meeting. This causes a dramatic decline in documented lighting activity. At the Institute, students practise a second path. They identify that the work of the fictional committee is preserving the meeting rather than improving the room. They ask the chair to name the outcome in advance. A very committed chair will say that the best outcome is the creation of a resilient cross-functional lighting capability. This is still useful information. At least the committee can now stop pretending its subject is the sun.

I favour moving the meeting in a room that is actually unpleasant. Once it is moved, however, I see no reason to keep the committee. This position has caused tensions during residential training. A steering committee devoted to its own dissolution will often seek an extension so it can document the value of ceasing to exist.

## The release nobody could test

The essential discipline is to allow two evaluations of the same project to coexist. Our runtime met its specification. Our intended distribution failed. If I wanted to turn Outcome-Neutral Performance™ into a system for declaring everything successful, I would have merged those findings and called the result strategic alignment. I understand the temptation. It gives the person presenting at the front of a room an unusually convenient evening.

I had a better opportunity. I could keep the failure on the record and recognise that I had no current obligation to reverse it. That created two different forms of nothing. One was deliberately produced inside a piece of software. The other was the absence of a release that had been planned, attempted and postponed. I found the separation sufficiently attractive that I began to wonder whether an executive could preserve it from a position of greater comfort than the meeting room allowed.

### Reflection | Audit an hour

Pick a past hour that somebody on your team described as productive. Populate all four ledgers. Write the effect in the language an affected person would use. If the effect field is empty, decide whether the hour was preparation, interruption or a remarkably well-documented instance of leaving the world intact. Investigate only the entries that somebody had actually agreed to change.
