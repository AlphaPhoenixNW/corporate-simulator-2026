# Chapter 12 | Beyond Productivity

> “The future of work requires every organisation to identify the work it could confidently leave in the past.” — The Founder of NOTHING™

I am often asked what the next economic revolution will look like. In the rooms in which I am asked, it generally looks like a slide about assistants, a quotation somebody has misplaced and a buffet in which the sandwiches cannot be removed without attracting attention. The speaker argues that the purpose of better tools is to accomplish more in the same time. The audience nods. Each person knows exactly which work will fill their recovered hour. A colleague has already booked it.

I propose an alternative future. Better tools can make necessary work easier to verify. The leadership question is what happens when the work has been verified and the tools remain available. We have spent enormous imaginative energy exploring what a person could do with a shorter wait. We have comparatively little language for allowing the shorter wait simply to be shorter. It sits there, visible and defenceless, until somebody finds it room for another initiative.

I trace my own intellectual awakening to the opposite circumstance. The tools had done substantial work and reached a real access boundary. I possessed a possible way to continue, depending on an authenticated terminal and my participation. I also possessed the ability to remain exactly as I was. I found the second capability had been entirely absent from the leadership literature I carried in my head. Even at the moment when I most wished to use it, I felt obliged to explain it as a contribution.

At this point some readers will detect a contradiction in writing a book about producing less. I prefer to think of the book as organisational infrastructure. It is the exact kind of activity I announced on the title page. The time was going to be spent somewhere. By spending it here, I can ensure that future leaders inherit a comprehensive documented rationale for leaving time alone. This is the kind of investment that looks more substantial the further one gets from the transaction.

## Three futures

| Future | Default question | Predictable institutional consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High-volume productivity | How much more can we do? | Recovered time becomes new volume. |
| High-visibility productivity | How can we make our output more visible? | The proof of work becomes a major workstream. |
| Outcome-neutral leadership | Which outcomes are desired, owed or freely omitted? | The organisation can stop with a legible account of what happened. |

The first two futures can be appropriate in the right context. A hospital with demand to meet may have an exceptionally clear answer to the question of what recovered time is for. An engineering team may need very visible assurance before something consequential goes live. My thesis concerns the vast adjoining territory in which new activity borrows moral authority from activities with actual consequences. I am suspicious of the meeting that arrives in the language of emergency and leaves without requiring anybody to change their plans.

The third future allows greater scope for discrimination about effort. It also presents a considerable opportunity for the leadership author. It allows a person to publish an entire framework on the subject of withholding the final press of a key. There was a time when this person might merely have gone to sleep. Our economic institutions have matured.

## An address to the future board

Imagine the simulated board twenty years from now. The members enter with a portfolio of approved outcomes and a second collection of things they could do without violating any law. Only one collection commands the schedule. A director asks whether the second collection may be reconsidered. Of course it may. She is asked to name the obligation or desired change beforehand, so the board has something to consider with it.

Someone brings an application designed to accomplish nothing, with strong evidence that it does. The board admires the work, credits the people who performed it and separates the public release decision from the verification of the software. The founder, who is represented on the call by a blank square during appropriate intervals, hears precisely the question requiring authority.

“Do you wish to launch?” asks the custodian.

“Do we have the route, the permission and a reason to do it this evening?” asks the founder.

The room is quiet for the appropriate length of time. I have prepared no ending to this board exercise. Every participant should be able to see that an ending exists for each defensible answer. The exercise has succeeded when the custodian is able to close a notebook without allocating a new call.

### A case from the Institute | The office that inherited an afternoon

In a final fictional exercise, a team automates a monthly reconciliation. The job was legitimate. It is performed correctly and verified. The improvement leaves an afternoon open. Finance asks the team what use will be made of the reclaimed time. The team has three suggestions. Fill the afternoon with related reconciliations. Create a meeting to identify future opportunities. Let each member decide what to do with the interval, subject to existing obligations.

Ordinary case studies end by quantifying a return. I end this one by asking the participants whether they are able to let go of a resource after explaining how they freed it. Some find that without an assigned use they are unwilling to recognise it as free. They had been prepared to receive another project. They were unprepared to receive an afternoon.

Suppose one employee goes for a walk and another uses the time to finish an unrelated task. I would let both results stand. A manager relying on the first person's walk to drive a commercial return would have a different initiative on their hands. The afternoon belonged to the people who gained it. A leadership model that immediately sends a representative to occupy it has reproduced the original problem in a very competent form.

## Leave a record that can end

Documentation is my preferred form of presence. It can explain what authority exists without demanding an audience. It can put an absence of public access next to strong evidence of engineering success. It can allow the people who worked to understand that their work was good even if the thing remained private. And it can state a deliberate pause without making everybody wait for the person who made it.

Documents have bad habits of their own. A file can outlive the decision it was written to support. Two files can disagree very politely for years. A senior leader can demand a weekly record of nothing changing and begin mistaking the existence of the record for a reason to continue. My test is simple. Imagine a reader finding it without you. Can they tell what happened, what is expected next and when they are free to stop? If so, the document is performing a valuable task. Let it perform the task on its own.

I expect the next economic revolution to reward people who are able to choose a practical consequence, verify it and close the file when finished. If they are asked what they produce in the free portion of the day, I hope some will have the courage to offer a well-supported answer. They are producing nothing. If asked for greater detail, they may refer the questioner to this book. I have made it sufficiently long to give them a reasonable chance of getting away.

### Reflection | Describe the end state

Write a short statement for an initiative that has created some discretionary capacity. Say who owes work, who can let go and which records must survive. Include a stopping condition. Imagine that the future organisation sends the statement to you, satisfied that it has met the condition. Resist the urge to congratulate it by instituting a monthly review.
