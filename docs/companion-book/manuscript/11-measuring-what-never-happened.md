# Chapter 11 | Measuring What Never Happened

> “The first duty of a dashboard is to let the executive know how much further they can afford to recline.” — The Founder of NOTHING™

When people discovered I was building a management discipline around an unchanged practical state, they immediately asked what I intended to measure. This is a sophisticated institutional reflex. An activity whose effect is uncertain can become budgetable as soon as it has a percentage and two quarters of comparison. The numbers do not have to be particularly high. They have to occur in adjacent columns.

I did not want to let the absence of public availability become a performance measure for the product. A metric with that property would have been very popular on launch night and very dangerous the following morning. I wanted fictional indicators that capture defined decisions inside named windows. This gives a review something to examine and a narrator something unusually grand to say about a small table.

All figures in this chapter are illustrative inventions. None is a reported result of NOTHING™, an industry benchmark or financial evidence. When the Institute trains with them it keeps a conspicuous sheet of imaginary data. The fact that the sheet contains a percentage has already caused concern in a simulated audit.

## The measurement contract

For all five ratios, count the eligible events and the events that satisfy the numerator, then compute a percentage. A zero denominator means **N/A**. An empty list can show that a team had no eligible case. It cannot show perfect performance in all eligible cases, a conclusion I once attempted to market before someone asked for the list.

Write the window and eligibility in advance. Write them sufficiently clearly that another person can recognise a marginal case without consulting the chairman. Do not remove events from the denominator because they look inconvenient next to a target. That practice produces a dashboard of exceptional serenity and remarkably low use.

| Indicator | Fictional formula | Eligibility and evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zero Outcome Delivery Rate (ZODR) | 100 × verified zero-outcome executions ÷ eligible planned zero-outcome executions | Count scheduled executions with an approved zero-outcome contract. Absence of an observed execution does not satisfy the numerator. |
| Executive Movement Avoidance Index (EMAI) | 100 × eligible requests resolved without additional physical movement ÷ eligible requests | Count requests for which movement was optional. Accept a recorded handling decision within the window. |
| Strategic Horizontal Alignment (SHA) | 100 × reviewed decisions satisfying the horizontal criteria ÷ reviewed decisions | Require a recorded authority, disposition and next trigger, with nobody left waiting for unstated instructions. |
| Unnecessary Action Suppression Ratio (UASR) | 100 × eligible proposals retired before execution ÷ proposals assessed and confirmed unnecessary | Define unnecessary after checking commitments and impact. Count an explicit retirement. |
| Stakeholder Expectation Neutrality (SEN) | 100 × reviewed audience groups with no mismatch between stated commitments and actual status ÷ audience groups with evidence for review | Count only supported audience observations. An imaginary group belongs on a labelled training sheet. |

ZODR needs careful handling. At launch time one could report excellent performance for the programme on local executions that met the contract. A distribution exercise without its own execution could not ride into the numerator on those runs. We could write down both the state of the programme and the state of public availability. The board could display them two centimetres apart and everybody would still know which one they were reading.

EMAI has an exceptional disadvantage as a real measure. It can lead a team to concern itself with movements that belong entirely to the individual. In our fictional model the requests are counted at the point of work and no physical monitoring is undertaken. A self-reported decision suffices. We once considered a sensor under the mattress. The finance department valued its potential insights and asked whether it would require anyone to get up to install it. The proposal did not survive the question.

SHA initially confused engineering, which already had perfectly good reasons to use those letters. In the training pack **SHA** names the alignment indicator. A commit hash remains a technical identifier. If a report says the release SHA is 83%, get the owner of the slide and the owner of the repository into the smallest meeting in which the problem can be resolved.

UASR can be gamed by proposing many things that should obviously never happen, then retiring them. To keep the exercise defensible, I require a review of proposals already made through normal channels. This prevents managerial colleagues stockpiling frivolous instructions simply to enjoy denouncing them at quarter end. I have seen the temptation. It is hard to resist an unlimited supply of things one can be seen deciding against.

SEN measures consistency between promises and observed status. In a separate simulated sample, four consistent commitments out of five reviewed audience groups would yield 80%. The exercise below begins with no such groups. Real investors were never part of the incident. If there are no evidenced groups in an actual exercise, use N/A. I admire the economy of a metric that, handled responsibly in our own case, can decline to offer a value at all.

## A sample performance dashboard

The following counts cover one fictional exercise window. The board must read the denominator before discussing the numerator.

| Indicator | Numerator / denominator | Display | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ZODR | 7 / 8 | 87.5% | Seven observed zero-outcome runs. One planned run provided no verification. |
| EMAI | 9 / 10 | 90.0% | One eligible request incurred additional movement. |
| SHA | 5 / 6 | 83.3% | One reviewed decision left a team waiting for an unstated trigger. |
| UASR | 3 / 4 | 75.0% | Three unnecessary items were retired; one went ahead before retirement. |
| SEN | 0 / 0 | N/A | No evidenced audience group entered the exercise. |

Here is how I read the dashboard. Engineering needs to look at the run it could not verify. The six decisions include one item that left other people waiting, so the action is to explain the trigger to the team. The movement figure can remain where it is unless we learn the tenth request reveals a process dependency. The suppression figure deserves a brief examination of what allowed the fourth proposal through. The senatorial-looking blank in the last row is an honest representation of evidence the organisation does not have. I refuse to shade it green.

### A case from the Institute | The remarkable quarter

Two fictional executives bring dashboards to a simulated committee. One shows perfect results across every metric. The second shows work with an 83.3% alignment reading and an unreported stakeholder figure. The committee congratulates the first executive. I ask to see their denominator. They have treated everything the company did as eligible where it succeeded, and nothing as eligible where it failed.

The second executive presents the sixth decision, names the team that had been left waiting and proposes a sentence to release the blockage. I ask the committee which executive has produced something worth reading. Several members change their votes. The first executive asks whether she can count the vote changes as excellent engagement with her original dashboard. The committee adjourns within its allotted time, an outcome I regard as independently significant.

### Reflection | Calculate an honest blank

From the sample dashboard, change the eighth run to a verified zero-outcome execution. Calculate ZODR again. Next change the rules so that no audience was available to review and show exactly how you report SEN. Explain why the first can become 100% and the second cannot. Close the dashboard after naming one action it supports. If your favourite action is to redesign the dashboard, consider leaving it in the drawer for a quarter.
