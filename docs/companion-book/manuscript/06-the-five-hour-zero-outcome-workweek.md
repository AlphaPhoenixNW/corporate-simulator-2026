# Chapter 6 | The Five-Hour Zero-Outcome Workweek

> “An hour left empty is vulnerable. An hour reserved for retaining its original state has the full protection of the calendar.” — The Founder of NOTHING™

The phrase *workweek* traditionally describes a period in which work is expected to occur. I use it to describe the logical space in which a commitment can be examined before anybody makes it. This space need not occupy five consecutive days. In my method it occupies five hours. Establishing the method may occupy considerably more, but those hours belong to implementation and should be classified elsewhere.

This is how I arrived at the question that appears on the title page. Why spend five hours achieving something when you can spend five hours building the organisational infrastructure required to achieve nothing? Read it slowly. The two uses of *five hours* refer to the same expenditure. The second creates a repeatable capability and is therefore eligible for a launch meeting.

I encourage you to imagine a week in which no committed work is already due. This is important. A person carrying an actual obligation should complete it or arrange a responsible deferral. My programme is designed for that portion of the calendar to which everybody has access until somebody writes a clear reason to occupy it. This is also the portion most heavily defended by people who enjoy issuing calendar invitations.

## The five-hour calendar

| Hour | Appointment | Boundary | Evidence at the end |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Inventory of Possible Outcomes | Write down outcomes somebody could reasonably expect | A list of commitments and optional ambitions |
| 2 | Elimination of Optional Activity | Examine optional work before anything goes to scheduling | An amended list with reasons and owners |
| 3 | Delegation Review | Route verification and preparation to their proper owners | A handoff for any task retained |
| 4 | Interval of Protected Non-Participation | Keep declined work off the calendar | No meeting, no preparatory call and no apology for the slot |
| 5 | Containment and Close | Verify what was deferred and return the remaining capacity | A short disposition record |

Hour one works best when people are permitted to mention things that will become embarrassing by hour two. A manager might say they wanted a cross-functional review. Ask which decision it would make. If they describe several people sitting inside a room, accept that as valuable information about the proposed format. Ask the question again. A finite account of the expected consequence will either emerge or leave a usable silence.

Hour two is when the organisation discovers how often it has used plausibility as permission. Plausible tasks are everywhere. Most objects could be reviewed, most processes could be benchmarked and many rooms would support a discussion about the conditions in which somebody should review them. I treat plausibility as a qualification for an idea list. I reserve the calendar for obligations, decisions and work with a named reason to occur.

Hour three subjects the remaining work to the test of load. I ask the executive to imagine going through the entire plan without touching a keyboard. At the precise point where the plan can proceed no further, decide whether the obstacle is authority or habit. Authority can be described and assigned within clear limits. Habit may require a team to discover that a document can come into existence without an executive looking at it half way through.

Hour four is frequently the most difficult in practice. Many leaders, finding no appointment, create one called *Deep Work*. A colleague notices that a block has appeared and offers to join it. The leader accepts this in a spirit of collaboration. The protected interval has now been converted into a double booking without a single disagreement. My recommended defence is to state the purpose of the interval in plain language and then give the hour a chance to elapse.

Hour five is evidence that I have observed the conventional finality of time. You will be tempted to build an ongoing process to guarantee that the five hours have finished. A calendar is already adequate for this purpose. Review the disposition and close the session. If somebody asks whether you would like the same slot next week, ask for the circumstance that would justify reacquiring it.

## My indicators for the week

I keep the dashboard deliberately small. These operational indicators describe the schedule. Later I will demonstrate how to make separate governance metrics sufficiently impressive for a board.

| Indicator | Count it when | Example from a fictional week |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Commitments honoured | A genuine obligation was assigned and handled | The team delivered its promised internal handoff |
| Optional demands retired | An owner explicitly withdrew a proposed activity | A recurring preview was cancelled |
| Hours redeployed | Time returned to people without another mandatory use | One hour released, with no impact calculation |
| Reopened items | An agreed trigger actually occurred | An authorised operator became available |
| Calendars reviewed recursively | A new meeting audited the existence of the original meetings | One proposed, zero approved |

An hour returned is an hour a person can decide how to use. I resist immediately converting it to a saving in money or output. In a fictional workshop, an accounts representative once multiplied a reclaimed block by the number of people invited and supplied a figure large enough to justify employing a person to reclaim more blocks. I admired the ambition. I asked whether anybody had verified that the now uninvited people planned to create a financial return during the hour. The figure was returned to its owner for reflection.

### A case from the Institute | The elastic Tuesday

A fictional publishing office has a Tuesday editorial stand-up. Everybody knows the manuscript has been sent to copy-editing. By long custom the group holds the meeting until it hears that the manuscript has returned. One editor proposes suspending the meeting while copy-editing is in progress. Another suggests a short transition session about how to suspend it. A third wants a status document so people can tell when the suspension starts.

The director tests the proposed work against hour two. Only one fact would change anybody's conduct. The manuscript has either returned or it has not. Give the copy editor a clear reporting route and let the Tuesday appointment go. The group adopts this plan. During the reclaimed slot, one editor reads a book. We examined whether this made the reclaimed time insufficiently outcome-neutral. The book was unrelated to work and the editor enjoyed it privately. The Institute considers this a tolerable incident of independent reading.

## The calendar ceremony

A senior calendar makes private ambitions look like the weather. Things appear in coloured rectangles and the executive arranges a life around them. I advise starting with a small ceremony. Print a representative week. Mark each commitment with the name of the person who would experience a relevant consequence if it were missed. Where nobody can be named, put the invitation aside. You may discover several hours in which organisational activity was merely waiting to be observed by someone with a title.

Do not book those hours to prove the ceremony worked. That is like returning a quiet room to an events department and asking it to demonstrate silence by putting on a show. An empty rectangle is a legitimate operational state. From a sufficient distance it resembles a well-made bed. I regard this resemblance as part of its instructional value.

### Reflection | Reserve a defensible absence

Try the calendar on an optional initiative. Record the real obligations first. Decide what to retain, retire or delegate. Leave the fourth hour genuinely open and identify the trigger for any work that could reappear there. At the end, write a one-paragraph disposition. Fold it in half and see if the essential information still fits on the visible side.
