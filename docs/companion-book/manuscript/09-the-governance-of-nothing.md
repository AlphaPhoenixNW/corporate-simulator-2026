# Chapter 9 | The Governance of Nothing

> “We achieved complete visibility of the release by declining to make the release visible.” — The Founder of NOTHING™

At a certain point after our launch failed, I realised that I could reconstruct the event without asking anybody to remember it. There was an incident record. It had a classification, an identifier and a finding. There was a risk register containing the possibility that I might favour comfort over deployment. There was a private draft statement, a proposed posture for a simulated board and a roadmap from which a delivery commitment had been carefully extracted. A comparatively small act had become legible to a company with the administrative habits of a maritime authority.

This is the central achievement of governance. It allows an organisation to encounter its problems in a format that can be circulated. A problem described poorly can consume a whole morning merely being rediscovered by people who were present yesterday. A problem described well can be considered, assigned a number and stored in a place where everybody can find it again if it decides to become relevant.

The danger, of course, is that circulation can become work in its own right. Having discovered the grace of a single well-governed incident, a leader can be tempted to subject ordinary inconveniences to the same treatment. I regard the temptation as evidence of real professional enthusiasm. I allow myself to indulge it here with a few controls.

## Five records, five jobs

| Record | Question it answers | What it should resist becoming |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Incident | What happened, in what order, and with what impact? | A marketing account of events |
| Risk register | What could still happen and which control would matter? | A character reference |
| Action register | What could change a recurrence, and under which trigger? | Evidence that actions have already been completed |
| Board memorandum | What posture is proposed for oversight? | Minutes of a meeting that never took place |
| Roadmap | Which ambitions carry a delivery commitment? | A subtler way of promising everything |

With NOTHING™, the incident established that the product worked and public distribution did not. It recorded the first-branch and authentication blockers and then the founder's decision to postpone. The roadmap converted this result into planning language. Public launch remained an aspiration. It would require a new owner decision. Anybody reading the roadmap after the incident could understand why there was no date against it. Anybody reading the incident could see that the roadmap's elegance had been purchased with an actual failure.

The board memorandum was a proposed position for a simulated board. It was not a vote. A company can acquire a distressing number of approvals by allowing verbs such as *endorsed* and *recorded* to do work for which there are no minutes. I prefer to preserve the modest shape of our governance. We had a founder, a proposal and a register. The register was meticulously written. At no point did it develop voting rights.

## The handling of R-001

The risk register ranked the possibility that the founder might prefer comfort over deployment as R-001. I was extremely pleased to be the first risk in the organisation. This confirmed my centrality to the operating model with a clarity unavailable through an ordinary biography. I was also pleased to discover that a risk can remain open without anybody insisting on changing one's personality.

Risk entries should describe exposure. The risk of my preferring comfort becomes a distribution problem only when the project has scheduled a release and the final action depends on me. If no release has been committed, the organisation can recognise the preference and decide whether it minds. A person becoming horizontal in a room disconnected from any obligation need not be given a criticality rating. There are easier ways to make them uncomfortable.

The proposed control was sensible. Prepare a clear handoff, confirm an authorised alternative when one is available and keep publication under owner control. An alternative operator could move an approved private bundle with the proper access. This would have separated the architecture of the release from the architecture of my spine. It would still have left me with a real decision at the last gate. I would expect governance to preserve that decision in whichever posture I happened to encounter it.

## Actions that remain actions

Our incident recorded deferred and proposed actions. Confirm the authorised operator when release planning reopens. Verify the exact root commit, history, privacy and destination checks after a successful import. Consider adding an access preflight the next time a release is planned. Keep public launch off the committed plan while there is no delivery decision.

This looks like a list of activities. Its chief virtue is actually the grammar. Some actions belong to a future condition, some are proposals and one describes the present roadmap. Treat them all as in progress and you immediately create the appearance of a new programme. Treat them all as completed and you acquire a release that nobody can find. Keep their different standings legible and the reader can close the document without opening a new task.

I call this **HOLD**, which is short enough to write on a small card at the end of a review.

| Step | Question |
| --- | --- |
| History | What was actually observed? |
| Ownership | Who has authority for any remaining action? |
| Limit | What are we explicitly declining to do today? |
| Decision | What evidence or instruction would change that position? |

HOLD is particularly useful at the point when a meeting begins to feel successful enough to schedule a follow-up. I have discovered that the warm feeling of a well-run review is one of the least reliable indicators that there is more work to do. It often means only that people enjoyed understanding the same thing at the same time.

### A case from the Institute | The misplaced adapter

In an illustrative company a presentation cable is mislaid. The group borrows a replacement and the talk proceeds. A committee classifies the missing cable as a strategic near miss and begins a review of leadership preparedness. A junior colleague asks what harm occurred. None was observed. She asks what change would prevent recurrence. The venue could keep a spare in a marked drawer.

At this point the committee has enough information to close the case. It instead begins a discussion about whether drawers need a policy. I offer the class ten minutes to design the smallest governance response compatible with the facts. The best answer is one person labelling one drawer. If a student produces a board pack, the simulated board asks them to find the cable it is now sitting on.

The lesson applies even to our more ambitious incident. There is a point at which the facts can be found, the owner is identified and the consequences are accepted. Beyond that point, the next document should earn its existence. I make an exception for entire books written to explain the discovery. A leader must retain sufficient discretion to protect the work on which their reputation currently depends.

### Reflection | Close an honest record

Take one incident that reached a defensible stop. Draft five lines for the five records above. Mark every proposed action with a real trigger. Add the name of the person who can declare that the issue is settled for now. Review the material once. Imagine how it will look in the morning when the business is still in the same state and nobody needs to ask you what happened.
